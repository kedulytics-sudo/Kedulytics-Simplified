Data Entry

Data entry is the process of transferring collected information into a digital format for analysis. Accurate data entry is essential because errors, inconsistencies, and missing values can compromise the quality and reliability of statistical results. Before analysis, ensure that variables are correctly coded, values are entered consistently, and the dataset is checked for errors. Using appropriate data-entry templates, validation rules, and double-checking procedures can help minimize mistakes and improve data quality.

Remember: Quality statistical analysis begins with accurate data entry.

Quantitative Data Diagnostics: Parametric and Non-Parametric Approaches

An important step in analyzing quantitative data is to examine its distribution and assess whether the assumptions required for the intended statistical method are reasonably satisfied. This process, known as data diagnostics, helps researchers select appropriate analytical techniques, produce reliable results, and draw valid conclusions.

Quantitative data can be analyzed using parametric or non-parametric methods, depending on the research question, study design, type of variable, and assumptions underlying the analysis. Parametric methods typically rely on assumptions about the population distribution or model errors, whereas non-parametric methods generally require fewer distributional assumptions. However, non-parametric methods are not assumption-free.

1. Parametric Analysis

Parametric methods are commonly used when their underlying assumptions are reasonably satisfied. These assumptions may include normality of model residuals, independence of observations, homogeneity of variance, and linearity, depending on the statistical method.

Examples include:

  • Independent-samples and paired-samples t-tests

  • Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)

  • Pearson correlation

  • Linear regression

Before applying these methods, researchers should consider the data structure, sample size, outliers, and relevant assumptions. For example, in a t-test or ANOVA, the normality assumption primarily concerns the errors or residuals within groups, rather than requiring all observed data to be normally distributed. With sufficiently large samples, some parametric procedures are reasonably robust to moderate departures from normality, although this depends on the design and the severity of the departure.

2. Non-Parametric Analysis

Non-parametric methods are useful when the assumptions of a corresponding parametric method are not adequately satisfied, particularly when working with ordinal data, highly skewed distributions, or data containing influential outliers. They can also be appropriate when the research question concerns ranks or distributions rather than means.

Examples include:

  • Mann–Whitney U test as an alternative to the independent-samples t-test

  • Wilcoxon signed-rank test as an alternative to the paired-samples t-test

  • Kruskal–Wallis test as an alternative to one-way ANOVA

  • Spearman rank correlation as an alternative to Pearson correlation when a rank-based association is appropriate

These methods do not all test exactly the same hypotheses as their parametric counterparts. For example, the Mann–Whitney U test compares distributions and can be interpreted as a test of differences in location under additional assumptions; it is not automatically a test of differences in medians.

3. Why Data Diagnostics Matter

Checking the data before analysis helps researchers:

  1. Select the appropriate statistical method: Determine whether a parametric procedure is suitable or whether a non-parametric alternative or another model is more appropriate.

  2. Identify unusual observations: Detect potential data-entry errors, extreme values, or influential observations that may affect results.

  3. Assess relevant assumptions: Examine normality, variance patterns, independence, linearity, and other assumptions required by the chosen method.

  4. Improve interpretation: Avoid applying statistical tests mechanically when their assumptions or hypotheses do not match the research question.

  5. Strengthen the credibility of findings: Make analytical decisions that are transparent, justified, and reproducible.

4. Practical Steps for Diagnosing Quantitative Data

A useful diagnostic process includes:

  • Visual inspection: Use histograms, density plots, boxplots, and Q–Q plots to examine distribution shape, outliers, and departures from normality.

  • Numerical summaries: Examine means, medians, standard deviations, interquartile ranges, skewness, and other relevant descriptive statistics.

  • Formal testing: Where appropriate, use tests such as the Shapiro–Wilk test to assess evidence against normality. Interpret these tests alongside graphical diagnostics because their sensitivity depends on sample size.

  • Assumption checking: Assess the assumptions relevant to the intended method, such as homogeneity of variance for certain group-comparison procedures.

  • Method selection: Choose the parametric method, non-parametric alternative, or another suitable approach that best fits the data, research question, and study design.

Important: Do not automatically choose a non-parametric test simply because a normality test is statistically significant. Consider the sample size, graphical evidence, robustness of the intended method, presence of outliers, and the scientific question. Depending on the situation, a transformation, robust method, generalized linear model, or bootstrap approach may be more suitable.

Ultimately, data diagnostics are not just a preliminary technical exercise. They are a fundamental part of responsible statistical analysis, helping researchers determine which method to use, why it is appropriate, and how its results should be interpreted.
